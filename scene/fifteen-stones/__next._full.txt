14:T7cf,Somewhere around 1500, in a temple in northwest Kyoto, somebody laid out a rectangle of raked gravel about the size of a tennis court. In it they set 15 stones, in 5 groups, on cushions of moss. Then they stopped. There are no trees inside the wall, no flowers, no pond and no path. You are not meant to walk in it. The veranda of the abbot's hall runs along one long side, and the garden is designed to be looked at from a sitting position, like a scroll that happens to be made of rock.

The famous property is a piece of arithmetic. From any point along the veranda, exactly one of the 15 stones is hidden behind another, so a viewer can only ever count 14. Move, and a different stone disappears. In the lunar calendar 15 is the number of nights to a full moon, and the traditional number for completeness. So the garden is understood to be holding back that wholeness on purpose, and to do it from every seat on the veranda. Nobody wrote down whether the designer intended that or whether centuries of visitors found it afterward. Almost nothing about the garden was written down. The designer is unknown, the date is a range, and two names are scratched on the back of one stone. They belong to men of low social class who may have been the labourers, or may have been the artists.

The rest of what is said about it is interpretation and always has been. The stones have been called islands in an ocean, a tiger carrying cubs across a river, and mountaintops above cloud. Zen temples of the period were also deeply political places, tied to the Ashikaga rulers in a city that had recently burned in the Ōnin War. The walls of Ryōan-ji went up while the country was falling apart. The garden's oil-clay wall, stained in ochre streaks by the seepage of centuries, is now admired as a deliberate effect. It is mostly age.

The gravel is raked by monks, in ridges that follow the shape of the stones, and the pattern is remade whenever it rains hard enough to spoil it, which in Kyoto is
