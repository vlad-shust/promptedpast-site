The Age of Sail · 14 February 1779

The Bay at Breakfast

The Pacific's greatest navigator misreads one crowd

look closer

Cook stands at the center in a clean white coat with both hands on his musket. He is looking the wrong way. The man behind him raises an iron dagger that was traded off Cook's own ships.

James Cook had spent 11 years crossing the Pacific and had a settled method for mornings that went wrong. Take the highest-ranking man in reach aboard as a guest-hostage, treat him honorably, and give him back when the stolen boat or musket came back. It had worked in Tahiti, in Tonga, in New Zealand. On the morning of 14 February 1779 at Kealakekua Bay in Hawaii, a ship's cutter was missing, and Cook went ashore with nine marines to collect the aging king Kalani'opu'u in the usual way. The visit had already curdled by then. The ships had first arrived during the Makahiki festival and been received with a lavishness that still puzzles historians. Then they sailed away, broke a foremast in a storm, and came back in the wrong season: a fleet of hungry strangers who had used up their welcome and returned for more. The king came along agreeably enough until his favorite wife and two chiefs pleaded with him at the water's edge, and a crowd began to gather and arm. Then a canoe brought word across the bay that English guns had killed a chief named Kalimu at the other blockade. The mood turned all at once. A warrior threatened Cook with a dagger of traded iron, the ships' own metal come back with an edge. Cook fired birdshot, which rattled off the man's war mat and proved the guns harmless. The crowd surged. Cook killed a man with his second barrel, the marines fired one volley, and the water's edge became a melee. Cook was clubbed from behind, held under, and stabbed repeatedly in the shallows, along with four of his marines. He was 50 years old, and the entire event, from landing to the boats pulling out, had not taken an hour. What happened next confused his shipmates more than the killing. The Hawaiians treated the body as they treated their own highest chiefs: the flesh removed and burned with honors, the bones cleaned, distributed among ranking chiefs as objects of power. A parcel of remains came back to the ships days later, wrapped in a chief's feathered cloak. Lieutenant King noted the genuine grief of people who had, by their own lights, done everything correctly. The Resolution buried what it received in the waters of the bay. Among those watching was the ship's sailing master, a 24-year-old named William Bligh, who filed away his own conclusions about managing Pacific crews and would test them, catastrophically, 10 years on. George Carter painted the scene in 1783 as London wanted it: Cook dead center in a white coat, with the light on him and everyone else in shadow. No witness account puts him that calm. The painting's business was not evidence; it was the manufacture of a secular saint, and the assembly line it started, engravings, plays, waxworks, ran for a century.
