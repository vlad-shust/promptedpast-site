5:T9c8,Sometime around 1501 an old monk went up a hill in Tango, on the north coast near the Sea of Japan, to look down at a strip of land he wanted to draw. He was Sesshū Tōyō, and he was over 80. Below him lay Ama-no-Hashidate, a natural sandbar 3.6 kilometers long and thick with old pines, laid across the mouth of a bay so straight it looked built by hand. People called it the bridge of heaven; a later age would rank it one of the three finest views in Japan. Sesshū sketched it from above and carried the sketch home to make this picture.

He had spent a lifetime getting to the point where he could. He was born in 1420 into a samurai family named Oda and given to a temple as a boy. The story that stuck to him ever after is about a punishment there. Tied to a pillar for some misbehavior and left alone, he is said to have wept, then drawn a mouse on the floor with a toe dipped in his own tears. The mouse was so lifelike that the monk who came to free him flinched from it. He trained in Kyoto at Shōkoku-ji, one of the great Zen temples. Then in 1468 he did what almost no established Japanese painter had done: he sailed to Ming China to study landscape painting at its source. He came back the most admired brush in Japan.

This is late work, made when he had almost nothing left to prove. It is ink on paper with only the faintest color, the whole scene built from wet grey washes and dry scratches of the brush. The pine bar floats in a pale bay. A town of temple roofs climbs the far shore. A mountain rises into mist with a monastery on its flank, and the temple’s name is written on the bare paper beside it. The picture carries no signature and no seal; it is given to Sesshū on the brush alone. You can date the painting by its buildings, which is a rare pleasure. The two-storey pagoda he drew at Chion-ji was finished in 1501. The halls he drew at the mountain temple burned in 1507. That pins the view to those six years, at the very end of his life.

What you are looking at is a real place, surveyed by an old man who went and stood there, not a Chinese ideal of mountains and water. Every roof is somewhere; the sandbar is still there today, still pined, and you can still walk it. The site is famous for a trick: stand at one end, bend over, and look at the bar upside down between your legs. Seen that way it seems to hang in the sky and earn its name. Sesshū skipped the trick. He climbed up and drew it the long, patient way, which is the only way he ever did
