The Age of Sail · c. 1680

The Cannon Shot

A warship burns money into a calm afternoon, and the smoke sits for its portrait

look closer

One gun has gone off. Its smoke is already wider than the ship that fired it, and there is no wind to take it away.

There is no battle. A Dutch warship has fired a single gun into a windless afternoon, a salute or a signal, powder burned for ceremony on a sea too calm to sail. The sound has already gone. What Van de Velde paints is the second afterward: sails hanging slack, a boat pulling away, and the smoke rolling off across water flat enough to hold the ship's reflection. Marine painters usually sold storms. Van de Velde knew the scarier boast was calm: a warship with two decks of guns and nothing to do, and a state rich enough to fire them at nothing. Powder was real money by the barrel, and salutes were budgeted like diplomacy, which is what they were. The state was seven small provinces behind a dike, and in the 1600s it ran, by contemporary reckoning, more merchant shipping than the rest of Europe together. Everything the Dutch Republic was floated: its grain, its spices, its credit, and its safety, which is why its war fleet mattered enough to have official artists. The Van de Veldes, father and son, were the firm. The father followed the battles in person in a small galliot, rowing between fleets under fire with a sketchbook, taking notes like a correspondent. The son turned the notes into oil. Between them they fixed how the age of sail would be remembered: the towering stern, the weather, the exact set of canvas, all of it accurate enough that historians still read their pictures like documents. At the battle of Solebay in 1672 the father worked from a hired galliot. He held position between two fleets that were trying to sink each other, drawing hull by hull while the shot went overhead. His sketchbooks survive, with the smoke noted in pencil. Clients could order their own ship into a painting the way a later age ordered portraits: the flags, the pennant numbers, the carved stern all correct, verified against the sitter. The calms the son loved were also the honest weather for that kind of accuracy. Nothing hides in a calm. Every spar and every vanity is on parade, doubled in the water underneath it. The ship gets painted from the stern quarter because the stern was the point. Carved, gilded and glassed, a seventeenth-century warship's stern was the state's face worn abroad, a floating credit rating. The gun that fired was part of the same language: salutes were counted, answered and argued over in treaties, honor measured out in gunpowder. Nations at sea spoke to each other in controlled explosions, and everyone understood the grammar. Then the firm changed flags. In 1672, the Republic's disaster year, France invaded and the market collapsed. The two greatest marine painters alive moved to London. Charles II, whose navy they had spent two wars documenting from the other side, gave them a studio in Greenwich and 100 pounds a year each. The royal warrant survives and splits the work like an invoice: the father paid for taking and making draughts of sea fights, the son for putting the said draughts into colours. They went on painting Dutch victories and English ones with the same brushes. The Cannon Shot hangs in Amsterdam now, left to the city by a collector and lent to the Rijksmuseum: one gun, no enemy, and the smoke taking its time.

Willem van de Velde the Younger, c. 1680
