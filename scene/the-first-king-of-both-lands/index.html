Ancient Egypt · c. 3100 BC

The First King of Both Lands

Two feet of siltstone carrying the oldest political announcement on earth

look closer

A fist is closed around a mace at the top of the swing, and the pear-shaped head sits above the knuckles. The fist is Narmer's. Ramesses II would be carved in the same stance at Abu Simbel 18 centuries later.

It is a cosmetic palette, the kind used for grinding malachite into eye paint, except that it is two feet tall and made of siltstone and nobody ever ground anything on it. The circular well on one side is formed by the entwined necks of two long-necked beasts, a design borrowed from Mesopotamia, and it exists to be looked at rather than used. British archaeologists found it in 1898 in a temple deposit at Hierakonpolis in Upper Egypt, buried with other ceremonial objects, and it has been the first page of Egyptian history ever since. One side shows a king in the tall white crown of Upper Egypt, mace raised, holding a kneeling enemy by the hair. The other shows the same king in the red crown of Lower Egypt, walking in procession past two rows of decapitated prisoners with their heads set between their feet. The message needs no translation, and the hieroglyphs above the figure give a name written with a catfish and a chisel, read as Narmer. Around 3100 BC, the argument goes, the valley and the delta became one kingdom under one man, and this object is the announcement. Egyptologists have spent a century pointing out how much the palette is doing besides reporting. Every convention that Egyptian art would use for the next 3,000 years is already fixed here. The king is twice the size of everyone else, the head in profile with the eye seen from the front, the scene locked into rigid horizontal bands, the arm raised to strike. Ramesses II would be carved in the identical stance at Abu Simbel 18 centuries later, which is like a modern state using a logo designed at the fall of Rome. Whether Narmer personally united anything is disputed; unification looks in the archaeology like a long process rather than an afternoon, and the palette may be a claim rather than a record. It is certainly a claim that worked. It sits now in the Egyptian Museum in Cairo, where its inventory number is written in ink inside the curl of a goddess's horn at the top left, small and black, in Arabic numerals. 5,000 years and two accession systems on, the object is still being catalogued.
