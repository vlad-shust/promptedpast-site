14:T916,Egypt had no coined money for most of its history and did not need it. The economy ran on grain. Wages were paid in it, taxes were assessed in it, temples were funded with it, and the whole machinery of the state existed to measure how much there was and where it went. The Nile flood arrived every summer, and the height it reached decided the year. A flood too low meant famine; too high meant washed-out villages and ruined seed. So the government measured the river with nilometers, stone staircases running down into the water with graduated marks, and set the tax rate from the reading before the harvest existed.

What that produced was the world's first paperwork civilization. Scribes counted the cattle, the fields, the sacks, the workers, the jars, the days of labor owed. The surviving tax registers, ration lists and delivery receipts outnumber the poetry by an enormous margin. A scribe was exempt from manual labor and knew it. The advice text known as the Satire of the Trades exists to say so. It runs through the misery of every other job: the potter grubbing in clay, the fisherman working beside crocodiles, the smith with fingers like crocodile hide. Each one ends on the same line: be a scribe.

The biblical story of Joseph is the outsider's version of that system. He is the foreigner who reads a dream about seven fat and seven lean years and is made overseer of the granaries. The story is accurate about the mechanism if not the man: store the surplus of good years, feed the country in bad ones, and end up owning the country. Famine records are real enough. One inscription on a rock at Sehel describes seven years of low flood under a king of the Old Kingdom. It was carved 2,000 years after the reign it claims to report, which is its own comment on how Egyptian bureaucracy handled an inconvenient past.

Alma-Tadema painted Joseph in 1874 on a panel 18 inches across, in a room built entirely from museum study. The wall decoration is lifted from the Nebamun tomb chapel in the British Museum, and the cartouche of Thutmose II, the oval that rings a royal name, sits on the side of the throne. A scribe on the floor holds a scroll open in both hands, with a heap of grain on the mat in front of him. The overseer looks past all of it.
