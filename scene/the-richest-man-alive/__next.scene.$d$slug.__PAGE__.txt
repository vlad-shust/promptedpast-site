5:Tc86,Croesus of Lydia had the kind of money that warps a story. Gold dust washed down the river Pactolus through his capital at Sardis. Herodotus credits the Lydians with the first true gold and silver coins, so the king could fairly regard wealth itself as a family invention. When Solon of Athens came through on his travels, Croesus had the treasuries opened for him, all of them. Then he asked his question: of all the men you have seen, who is the most fortunate? The answer was meant to be a formality.

Solon named Tellus of Athens, a man the king had never heard of. He lived decently in a decent city, and his children and grandchildren outlived him. He died old, fighting off a border raid, and was buried at public expense on the spot where he fell. Asked for a runner-up, Solon offered Cleobis and Biton, two young Argives who yoked themselves to an ox cart when the oxen ran late and hauled their mother five miles to the festival of Hera. Their mother prayed the goddess to give them the best thing a man can have; they lay down that night in the sanctuary and did not wake. Count no man fortunate until he is dead, Solon said; the luckiest man alive is one throw from ruin. Croesus, Herodotus writes, now took his guest for a fool, and sent him away.

The bill arrived itemized. His son died on a boar hunt, speared by the very guest the king had ritually cleansed of bad luck. Croesus trusted the oracle at Delphi because he had tested it. He had sent riders to ask what the king was doing on an appointed day, and Delphi alone answered right: boiling a tortoise with a lamb in a bronze pot. Now the oracle told him that if he crossed the river Halys a great empire would fall. He crossed, and the empire was his. Cyrus of Persia took Sardis in 546 and put the richest man alive on a pyre, and Croesus called one name three times, Solon, Solon, Solon. Cyrus asked what it meant, heard the story translated, and, depending on the version, a change of heart or a sudden rainstorm brought Croesus down alive to serve out his days as the conqueror’s advisor.

The interview is almost certainly invented. The travel dates of the real Solon and the reign of the real Croesus refuse to overlap. Plutarch checked, and wrote that he would not give up so famous a story to the so-called rules of chronology. The real Solon needs no legend anyway. As archon he canceled Athens’ debts, brought home citizens who had been sold abroad to cover them, and banned forever the loan secured on a person’s own body. Then he left town for 10 years so that nobody could talk him into undoing any of it. That trip is exactly how the storytellers got him to Sardis.

Honthorst painted the scene in 1624 in Utrecht, home a few years from Rome, where his candlelit pictures had earned him the name Gherardo delle Notti. His Croesus wears ermine and a jeweled stage crown, a servant displays embroidered cloth, and a man in chains kneels beside the silver; the canvas hangs in Hamburg. Cleobis and Biton, meanwhile, came up out of the ground in the 1890s, when French excavators at Delphi found two archaic marble brothers, generally taken for the pair the Argives dedicated.
